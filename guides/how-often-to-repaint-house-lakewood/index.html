How often should you repaint a Lakewood house?

What federal research says about paint life, what Lakewood’s sun and freeze-thaw do to those numbers, and the signs on your own walls that matter more than the calendar.

Get a free estimateCall 720-912-4676

Updated October 25, 2026 · By Bighorn Painting, Lakewood

Short answer: federal lab data puts a primer plus two good topcoats at roughly 10 to 20 years on vertical wood siding, and about half that with only one topcoat. Lakewood’s sun and freeze-thaw push most homes toward the shorter end, and the south and west walls get there first. Let those walls, not the calendar, decide.

What the research actually says

Most “repaint every 5 to 10 years” advice online comes from contractors and does not cite anything. The best neutral source we know of is the Wood Handbook from the USDA Forest Products Laboratory, which pulls together decades of outdoor exposure tests. Its numbers are for wood siding on a wall (not decks), in an average location in the lower 48 states.

Two things in that table matter more than the headline range. First, the coat count: a one-topcoat job buys you roughly half the life. Second, the handbook is clear that life “depends on climate and exposure,” and Lakewood is not an average location.

Why Lakewood homes land on the short end

More ultraviolet light

The EPA puts the rise in UV at about 6% for every kilometer of elevation. Lakewood sits more than a mile up, so a wall here takes roughly a tenth more UV than the same wall at sea level. UV breaks down the binder that holds paint together. That is what chalking is: the binder failing and leaving loose pigment behind. Our Golden team wrote a longer piece on why exterior paint fails faster at altitude, if you want the detail.

A lot of freezing and thawing

NOAA’s 1991–2020 normals for Denver International Airport, the nearest long-record station, show about 156 nights a year at or below freezing but only about 22 days that stay below freezing all day. That gap is roughly 134 days a year when water in a crack can freeze overnight and thaw by afternoon. Any open caulk joint or hairline crack gets worked open a little more each time.

Sun on one side, shade on the other

On a typical Lakewood house the south and west elevations bake all afternoon while the north side barely sees direct sun. That is why one wall can be chalking and peeling while the opposite wall still looks fine. It is also why painting the whole house on a fixed schedule can waste money on the sides that did not need it yet.

Lakewood’s housing stock changes the question

Census figures for Lakewood (ACS 2024, 5-year estimates) show that 55.8% of the city’s roughly 73,500 homes were built before 1980, and the median home was built in 1977. Most of those houses have been painted several times already. So the real question on an older Lakewood home is often less “how many years has it been” and more “are the old layers still stuck to the wall?”

When old paint starts to lift in sheets, adding another coat on top just adds weight to a failing bond. That house needs scraping and spot priming down to sound paint first, and the estimate should say so. On homes built before 1978, that prep has to follow federal lead-safe rules, which we cover in the questions below.

Signs it is time, whatever the calendar says

  • Chalk on your hand. Wipe a sunny wall with a dark cloth. Heavy powder means the binder is going.
  • Cracking or flaking in a grid or along board edges, especially on the bottom course and under windows.
  • Bare wood. This is the one not to ignore. In federal tests, wood left bare for 16 weeks before painting needed refinishing within 3 years, while boards painted after one week went 13 years before peeling began. Exposed wood is a clock that is already running.
  • Open caulk joints at corners, trim and window frames. These are water paths, and they are cheap to fix early.
  • Fading you can measure. Look behind a shutter, downspout or light fixture. If the protected patch is a different color from the open wall, the sun has been working.
  • Stucco clues: chalky coating, hairline cracks, or white mineral streaks below cracks. Our stucco guide explains what each one means.
  • Hail chips on trim and fascia after a storm. Document them before anyone paints; see what to do first after hail.

Can you repaint only the worst sides?

Often, yes. Repainting the south and west elevations a few years ahead of the rest is a reasonable way to spread out cost on a house whose north side is still sound. Two cautions. Paint the full wall from corner to corner, because a patch on a faded wall shows. And keep the color and sheen records from the last job, since the same named color from a newer batch can look slightly different next to eight-year-old paint.

If you are trying to budget either way, our page on what exterior painting costs in Lakewood lays out published per-square-foot ranges and what pushes a quote up or down.

Stucco, brick and decks run on different clocks

Stucco is not in the federal service-life tables, so watch the surface rather than a number. The coating choice matters as much as the timing because stucco has to dry out; see our stucco painting service page.

Unpainted brick does not need paint at all. Once brick has been painted, it needs a breathable masonry coating and goes on the same watch-the-walls schedule as everything else.

Decks and fences wear much faster than walls because they face the sky and hold snow. The handbook notes that film-forming finishes on decks often last no longer than penetrating stains, which are easier to renew. Our deck and fence staining page covers the usual cycle.

How to push your next repaint toward the long end

  1. Buy the full system. Primer on anything bare or repaired, then two topcoats, written into the estimate.
  2. Fix water first. Point sprinkler heads away from siding, keep gutters clear and add downspout extensions. Water at the bottom course undoes good paint fast.
  3. Wash it once a year. A gentle wash removes dirt and mildew that hold moisture against the film.
  4. Recaulk early. A tube of good sealant every spring is cheaper than a rotted fascia board.
  5. Touch up bare spots the same season you find them, before the wood starts to weather.
  6. Paint in the right weather. NOAA’s normals put the median last spring freeze at Denver’s airport around May 5 and the first fall freeze around October 9. The overnight low matters most, and every paint label sets its own minimum.

Questions

Common questions

How often should a house be painted in Colorado?

There is no honest single number. The U.S. Forest Products Laboratory puts a primer plus two top-quality topcoats at about 10 to 20 years on vertical wood siding in an average U.S. location, and about half that when only one topcoat goes over the primer. At Lakewood’s elevation, the sunny south and west walls usually wear out first. Check those walls every spring and repaint when the film starts to chalk, crack or open up, not when a set number of years has passed.

How long does paint last on stucco in Lakewood?

The federal service-life tables cover wood, not stucco, so be wary of anyone quoting an exact figure. Stucco tends to tell you when it is ready: the coating chalks onto your hand, hairline cracks open, or white mineral deposits appear below cracks. The right coating matters as much as the timing, because stucco has to let moisture out. Our stucco painting page covers when an elastomeric coating helps and when it traps water.

Is it OK to wait another year before repainting?

Often, yes. If the paint is still bonded and no bare wood is showing, waiting a season usually costs very little. The risk changes once wood is exposed. Federal tests found wood left bare for 16 weeks before painting needed refinishing within 3 years. Prices are rising too: the producer price index for paint was up 5.0% in the year to September 2026, and construction labor costs rose 3.5% in the year to mid-2026 (BLS).

Do dark exterior colors need repainting sooner?

They often look tired sooner. The Forest Products Laboratory notes that dark colors may fade within a few years even when the film is still sound, and dark paint runs hotter in direct sun. Fading is cosmetic at first, so a faded but intact dark wall can wait. Our guide to colors that survive Colorado sun explains which pigments hold up best.

Does one topcoat versus two really make a difference?

Yes, and it is one of the few places where federal research gives a clear number. The Wood Handbook says a primer plus a single topcoat lasts about half as long as the ideal system of a primer and two topcoats. When you compare bids, look for the coat count in writing. “Paint exterior” on a quote does not tell you which system you are buying.

Do I need to worry about lead paint on an older Lakewood house?

If the house was built before 1978, assume the older layers may contain lead until a test says otherwise. Federal law requires renovation work that disturbs more than about 20 square feet of exterior paint on those homes to be done by an EPA-certified firm with lead-safe practices. Colorado also requires the contractor to give you the EPA Renovate Right pamphlet at least 7 days before work that disturbs 2 square feet or more. Census data shows over half of Lakewood homes were built before 1980.

How do I check whether my paint is chalking?

Rub a clean, dark cloth or your palm across a sunny wall. A heavy white or colored powder means the binder is breaking down and the paint has stopped protecting the surface well. Light chalk is normal on older paint and washes off before repainting. Heavy chalk needs a thorough wash, and sometimes a bonding primer, so the new coat does not peel off with the powder.
