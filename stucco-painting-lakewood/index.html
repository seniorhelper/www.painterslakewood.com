Stucco breathes, and that changes everything

Traditional stucco is a cementitious surface applied over lath. It is porous by design. Moisture that gets into a wall assembly needs a way out, and in a stucco house a good part of that drying happens through the stucco itself.

Put a coating on it that does not allow vapor through, and the moisture has nowhere to go. It collects behind the film, and eventually it pushes the coating off in sheets, often taking a layer of stucco with it. That failure is not a paint defect. It is a specification error.

So the first question on any Lakewood stucco job is not color. It is what the wall needs to do.

We will tell you plainly which you have. Painting over the third kind hides a problem that gets more expensive the longer it is hidden.

Elastomeric: useful, oversold, and not always right

Elastomeric coatings are thick, flexible films that bridge hairline cracks and resist wind-driven rain. On the right wall they perform very well, and on a crack-prone west Lakewood elevation they are frequently the right call.

They are not a universal answer. Two honest caveats:

  • Permeability varies by product. Some elastomerics breathe adequately, others much less. On a wall with a moisture source, the wrong one traps water.
  • They are difficult to reverse. Once a thick elastomeric film is on, removing it is a serious project. Going on is a decision, not a maintenance step.

Any contractor who recommends elastomeric before looking at your walls is selling a product rather than solving your problem.

Synthetic stucco is a different animal

Some Lakewood homes, particularly certain 1990s builds, have synthetic stucco, an insulated system with a thin acrylic finish coat rather than traditional cement stucco. It looks similar from the sidewalk and behaves very differently. It is less permeable, more sensitive to moisture intrusion at penetrations, and it needs compatible coatings.

Identifying which you have is step one. Tap it: traditional stucco over lath sounds solid, synthetic over foam sounds hollow.

Where water actually gets in

On nearly every failed stucco wall we are called to look at, the water entered somewhere other than the field of the wall. Window and door perimeters with failed sealant. Deck ledgers. Roof-to-wall transitions. Penetrations for hose bibs, vents and light fixtures. Those get sealed as part of the work, because a perfect coating on a wall that is still taking water is a temporary result.

Questions

Stucco painting questions

Can stucco be painted?

Yes, with the right coating. Stucco is porous and needs to breathe, so the product is chosen for vapor permeability rather than film thickness alone. A coating that seals the wall completely can trap moisture and push itself off.

What is an elastomeric coating and do I need one?

It is a thick flexible film that bridges hairline cracks and resists wind-driven rain. It performs well on crack-prone walls, which many west Lakewood elevations are. It is not universal: permeability varies by product and it is hard to reverse once applied.

My stucco is cracking. Is that serious?

It depends on the pattern. Hairline spidery cracks across a wall are normal thermal movement. Straight cracks at window and door corners are common stress cracks. Wide, stepped or growing cracks need assessment before any coating goes on.

How do I know if I have synthetic stucco?

Tap it. Traditional stucco over lath sounds solid; synthetic stucco over foam board sounds hollow. The distinction matters because synthetic systems are less permeable and need compatible coatings.

How often does stucco need repainting?

Longer than siding in most cases, often eight to twelve years depending on exposure and coating. West-facing walls on the foothills edge reach that point sooner.

Do you repair the cracks or just paint over them?

Repair first, always. Hairline cracks are filled and bridged, stress cracks get flexible patching material, and penetrations are sealed. Coating over open cracks is cosmetic and short-lived.
